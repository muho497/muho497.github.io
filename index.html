// METHOD — FIVE MOVES
01 // ASSUMPTION LEDGER

Write down the given

Every division lists what it takes for granted. If a belief cannot be written, it cannot be tested — and it is running the division anyway.

02 // ELENCHUS

Cross-examine it

Adversarial questioning in the Socratic form: the claim, the strongest objection, what would falsify it, and the date it will be tested.

03 // FIRST-PRINCIPLES REBUILD

Derive it again

Rebuild the thing from physics, mathematics and economics as if the textbook did not exist. Where the rebuild diverges from the textbook, look there.

04 // ANOMALY HUNT

Collect what does not fit

The failed run, the outlier, the result nobody wanted. Anomalies are where the next model lives; a clean dataset is often a censored one.

05 // INVENTION LOG

File what falls out

What the inquiry produces is filed as a discovery note or an invention disclosure — and handed to a division with a name and a date, never left as an idea.

06 // ZETESIS ON ZETESIS

The method is on the ledger

The unit's own five moves are an assumption. If the ledger stops producing changed decisions, the method is questioned first.

      // THE PROBLEM

      DAMAGE NOBODY SEES

      More than 28,000 objects are tracked in orbit, of which only a few thousand are working satellites; everything else is debris. Below the tracking threshold sits a far larger population of micrometeoroid and small debris particles that cannot be catalogued at all — and that strike at velocities where millimetre-scale objects do real structural damage.

      Today, knowing whether a spacecraft has been hit means downlinking imagery and analysing it on the ground: bandwidth, delay, and a human in the loop for every frame. ORBITAL-1 moves the question on board. The obstacle is not the model — it is data: real photographs of hypervelocity impact damage on flight hardware are rare, scattered across archives, and nowhere near enough to train a detector. That data problem is what this project actually solves.

      // METHOD — SEVEN STAGES

      HOW THE DATASET IS MANUFACTURED

      Each stage is a separate module, so the corpus can be regenerated end to end rather than trusted on description alone.

      01

      Reference grounding

      Twenty curated photographs of real hypervelocity-impact and orbital-environment damage — LDEF returned hardware, Hubble solar-array sections, the ISS cupola window, and ground HVI test coupons, from NASA and ESA sources.

      WHY IT MATTERS → prompts are written against real photographs, not abstract description. Physical plausibility comes from the reference, not from imagination.
      02

      Parameter sampling factorial_sampler.py

      A combinatorial sampler spans seven variation axes under plausibility guards that reject physically inconsistent combinations. Roughly 13–15% of samples are generated as hard-negative confusers — surface features that look like damage but are not.

      WHY IT MATTERS → the confusers are what keep a detector from calling every scratch and shadow an impact.
      03

      Image synthesis FLUX.2 [klein] 4B

      Generation with native multi-reference conditioning under an Apache 2.0 licence. FLUX.2 requires natural-language prompting rather than keyword-dense strings, so the prompt library was rewritten from scratch.

      WHY IT MATTERS → the licence was chosen before the model: klein 4B is what makes a public dataset release possible alongside the paper.
      04

      Segmentation & compositing sam3_mask.py · composite_damage.py

      SAM 3.1 with Object Multiplex performs text-prompted segmentation, with a classical Otsu + Canny path as fallback. Damage is composited onto substrate imagery using Reinhard Lab colour-space matching and OpenCV seamless cloning.

      WHY IT MATTERS → labels fall out of the compositing step as pixel-exact geometry. No human draws a box on a synthetic frame.
      05

      Orchestration run_pipeline.py

      End-to-end runner with dry-run, resume and progress tracking.

      WHY IT MATTERS → a dataset nobody can regenerate is not a result. This is the reproducibility layer.
      06

      Two-stage training train_yolo26.py

      Stage 1 pre-trains on the synthetic corpus to learn general damage features. Stage 2 fine-tunes on real imagery with a frozen backbone and reduced learning rate.

      WHY IT MATTERS → synthetic data teaches the shape of damage; real data teaches what a real sensor sees. Neither alone is enough.
      07

      Evaluation eval_paper.py

      Produces the results table directly: mAP50, mAP50-95, precision, recall and edge latency, against the frozen real test set.

      WHY IT MATTERS → the ~20-image real test set is never touched during training or model selection. That constraint is treated as inviolable.

      // TAXONOMY

      TWO CLASSES, ON PURPOSE

      The taxonomy started at twelve categories across three groups and was deliberately collapsed to two. At this dataset scale, two well-populated classes train; twelve sparse ones do not. The finer taxonomy is kept for future work rather than discarded.

      CLASS 01

      impact_damage

      Craters, perforations, spallation and the surrounding disturbed zone — the signature of a hypervelocity strike on a surface.

      CLASS 02

      thermal_surface_damage

      Degradation of thermal control surfaces and coatings: discolouration, blistering, delamination — damage that is not impact but must not be confused with it.

      DELIBERATE NEGATIVES

      hard confusers

      13–15% of samples show surfaces that resemble damage without being damage. They exist so the detector learns to say no.

      One design note worth stating plainly: instance multiplicity beat volume. Putting two to four damage instances in a frame proved a higher-leverage lever than generating more frames — for this class count and this pipeline, it is the cheaper path to a harder dataset.

      Bounding-box labels for the thirteen-class real corpus do not exist yet. The paper's results therefore come from a YOLO11s-cls image classifier — whole-image damage classification without localisation. The synthetic corpus already carries pixel-exact boxes from compositing, so the detector path is blocked by annotation labour, not by method.
      Edge latency has not been measured on flight-representative hardware.
      Timing is measured on desktop hardware and reported alongside published reference figures for Jetson Orin NX-class devices. On-orbit latency figures are design targets, not results, and are labelled as such wherever they appear.
      The synthetic-to-real gap is real and only partly mitigated.
      Generation is anchored on twenty real references, which is a small set. Hard negatives and the frozen test set guard against over-optimistic evaluation, but residual distribution shift remains the principal threat to validity — and is precisely why the flight configuration includes a retraining loop.

      // WHERE LANGUAGE MEETS DIMENSION

      These two pillars are one question asked twice. Language encodes meaning in symbols; dimension encodes it in form. The same piece of knowledge travels a ladder of representations — a sentence (1D), a diagram (2D), a part or a printed object (3D), a mission or simulation unfolding in time (4D), a model's latent space (n-D) — and something is gained and lost at every rung. Nexus Apex treats that translation as a discipline rather than an accident: a paper becomes a UML figure, a figure becomes a CAD part, a part becomes a digital twin, and the twin has to be explainable back in a sentence. The frontier end of this — whether higher-dimensional structure is physically real or only a useful representation — is held openly as speculation, not doctrine.
